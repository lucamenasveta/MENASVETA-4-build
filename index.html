Freediving the Andaman Coast

A plain guide to freediving the Andaman coast: where to go, when to go, and how to stay safe doing it.

The Andaman Sea gives freedivers something the rest of the region rarely does: warm water, sheltered limestone bays, and vertical walls that drop past recreational depth within a short boat ride of shore. From Phuket you can be on a line in under an hour, and the same water that draws scuba boats by the dozen is usually empty twenty meters down and fifty meters to the side. This guide covers the sites worth the fuel, the months worth planning around, and the safety habits that separate a good session from an ambulance ride.

Where to Dive

Andaman freediving splits into three rough categories: sheltered bays for training and skill work, offshore pinnacles for depth, and wrecks for the divers who care more about what they are looking at than how deep they got. Most people starting out spend their first season entirely in the first category, and there is no shame in that. A calm bay with a fixed line teaches more in ten sessions than a dramatic pinnacle teaches in one.

Racha Noi

An hour and a half south of Chalong pier, Racha Noi has the clearest water within day-trip range of Phuket and a steep western wall that lets you hang a line to forty meters without drifting over a reef. Bring your own buoy. The dive boats that run here are set up for tanks, not for freedivers, and the good depth is a short swim off their mooring.

Koh Haa

Five islands arranged around a shallow lagoon in Krabi province, and the most forgiving depth site on this coast. The lagoon sits flat even when the outside is choppy, the walls start at ten meters and keep going, and the cathedral chamber on the northern island is worth a lungful of air and a slow look around.

King Cruiser Wreck

A car ferry that hit Anemone Reef in 1997 and now lies between Phuket and Phi Phi with its deck at about eighteen meters. Currents here are real and the visibility is moodier than the islands, so treat it as a site you visit once you are comfortable, not a place to practice equalizing.

Seasons and Conditions

The Andaman runs on two monsoons and they decide your year. From roughly November to April the northeast monsoon brings flat mornings, visibility that regularly passes twenty-five meters, and water sitting around twenty-nine degrees. From May to October the southwest monsoon turns the west-facing sites into washing machines, drops visibility to single digits after heavy rain, and closes some national park sites entirely. Plan depth work for the dry season and use the wet season for pool training, technique and fitness.

Conditions also change through the day. Mornings are usually glassier than afternoons, and on the outer islands the wind fills in around eleven. If you are paying for a boat, book the first departure and accept the early alarm. It buys you the better half of the day.

Reading a forecast before you commit

  1. Check swell height and period first. Anything over one and a half meters at the outer islands means a rough ride and a line that will not sit still.
  2. Check wind direction, not just speed. Ten knots from the east is a workable day at Racha; ten knots from the west is not.
  3. Check the rain radar for the twelve hours before your dive, because runoff kills visibility long after the sky clears.
  4. Check tide times if you are diving anywhere near a channel, since current at Anemone Reef and the King Cruiser tracks the tide closely.
  5. Call the boat operator the evening before. Local knowledge beats every app on this list.

Training and Safety

Freediving is unusually safe when the rules are followed and unusually unforgiving when they are not. Almost every serious incident in the sport traces back to the same short list of mistakes, and every one of those mistakes is avoidable by a diver who has taken a course and dives with a partner who has taken one too. If you are reading this as a first step toward getting in the water, the course comes before the gear.

Non-negotiables

  • Never dive alone, and never treat a boat crew or a nearby scuba group as your buddy.
  • Use one-up-one-down: one diver goes, the other watches the line and meets them at the last ten meters.
  • Do not hyperventilate before a dive. It delays the urge to breathe without adding oxygen, which is exactly how people black out.
  • Weight yourself neutral at ten meters, not at the surface, so a passive ascent works in your favor.
  • Do recovery breaths on surfacing and stay at the buoy for thirty seconds before you speak or move off.
  • Stop the session when equalizing turns difficult. Forcing it is how divers end a trip on day one with a squeezed ear.

Certification agencies teach broadly the same material with different depth targets. The AIDA International course syllabus is the most common route on this coast and is what most Phuket schools teach. Molchanovs Wave 1 leans harder on technique and on training between courses, which suits people who want structure after the certificate. If you prefer to start through an agency you already know from scuba, the PADI Freediver course covers the same fundamentals to sixteen meters. Whichever you pick, ask the school how many students share an instructor on the line, because that number matters more than the logo on the certificate.

Plan a Trip

A workable first trip is five days: two days of course work in a bay, one rest day, and two days on boats once your equalizing is holding. Base yourself in Rawai or Chalong on Phuket's south coast and you are ten minutes from the piers that run to Racha and Phi Phi. Book the course before you book the flights, since the good schools fill months ahead in high season and a half-empty low-season class is often the better experience anyway.

Budget for the boat, not the course. Course fees across the island sit in a narrow band and the schools compete on instructor quality rather than price, but day trips to the outer islands add up quickly, and a shared charter with three other divers costs a fraction of a private one. Post on the local freediving groups a week ahead and you will usually find people splitting a boat on the days you want.
